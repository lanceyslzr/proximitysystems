Try it yourself

Every question has two answers that look right.

That is the design of the exam, not an accident. Below is a prioritisation question. Pick the client you would see first, and I will show you how I read it.

Question 1 of 3

Options B and D above are both genuinely abnormal. If you are reading for what is wrong with the client, both qualify, and that is how a well-prepared student loses a question they actually understood.

What I teach is the order you read in, so the plausible answers fall away before they can tempt you.

  • Read the last line first. Find out what is being asked before the details crowd your thinking.
  • Name the question type. Priority, delegation, safety and teaching each have their own rule, and the rule does the work.
  • Eliminate on the rule, not the feeling. Airway before circulation. Unexpected before expected. Unstable before uncomfortable.
  • Check your answer against the question. Not against what you know. Against what it actually asked you.

It is a habit, not a talent. Most students have it within a few sessions, and it works on questions neither of us has ever seen.

Who I work with

Three people come to me most, and none of them is a reason to feel embarrassed.

You have taken it before

Sitting it again is a different problem from sitting it the first time, and it is not a content problem. We work on what actually happened in that room, and on the reading habit that let those questions slip past you.

You trained outside Canada

You have practised as a nurse, often for years. This exam tests a way of phrasing and prioritising that is not how you were taught it. That gap is learnable, and it says nothing at all about your ability as a nurse.

You freeze on the day

You answer correctly at your kitchen table and lose the same questions under the timer. A method you can still follow when your mind goes blank is worth more than another five hundred practice questions.
